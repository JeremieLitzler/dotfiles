When I feel good and ambitious, I can come up with a dozen goals and think I can work on all of them.

Can you relate to that?

- “I want to get in better shape.”
    
- “I should start a new business!”
    
- “Let’s travel more!”
    
- “I want to learn a new language.”
    
- “Maybe I should finally write that book.”
    

All good ideas.

The thing is, you can’t realistically do everything at the same time. And this is something we all know deep down.

But somehow we keep falling for the same mistake of always trying to do more, more, more.

You can pursue a lot of these goals over your lifetime, but each one needs more attention than whatever time you happen to have left.

And choosing is hard because you genuinely want all these things. Delaying one can feel like giving up on it. So you keep trying to fit everything in, even when you can see that it isn’t working.

In this guide, I’ll show you how to decide what needs your attention first, reduce the work around it, and plan a week you can actually follow. We’ll also look at how to tell whether your priorities are producing better results.

## 1. What needs to improve first?

When several areas of your life need attention, it’s tempting to make a big plan for all of them. A new workout routine, a career change, a side business, a savings goal, maybe a language course too.

I’d slow down a bit.

First, look at what will happen if you leave things as they are. Some things have real consequences soon. An unpaid bill, a deadline you’ve agreed to, or a problem at home that needs a conversation. Deal with those before adding another personal goal.

But don’t give every uncomfortable feeling the same urgency. Seeing someone else’s success and feeling behind doesn’t mean you need to start a business this afternoon.

Then ask yourself: What improvement would make the biggest difference to my life right now?

If your finances are a mess, getting a grip on your spending and income might take pressure off several other areas. If work takes up every evening, reducing that workload might give you time for your health and relationships. Look for the problem that keeps interfering with everything else.

You still need to look after the rest of your life. When I talk about [focusing on one thing](https://dariusforoux.com/one-thing/), I mean one major priority per area. You can work on a career goal and exercise. You don’t have to choose between having a job and being healthy.

But there’s a difference between maintaining a workout routine and training for your first marathon while you’re also changing careers and starting a company.

Decide which area needs extra effort this month. For the others, keep a simple routine you can maintain. You can push harder there later.

## 2. What result do you actually want?

“Work on my career” can mean almost anything. You could spend the whole weekend reading career advice and feel like you’ve done what you planned.

What do you actually want to change?

- If you want a new job, decide which kind of role you’re going after.
    
- If you need more income from your business, identify the product or service you want to sell.
    
- If you want to stop missing deadlines, choose the piece of work you need to deliver and agree on what finished means.
    

Now you can look at the work that might get you there.

This is also where you need to be honest about whose result matters. If you work for someone else, ask what they need from you. You can spend days making a beautiful presentation when your manager needs a recommendation they can act on. Find that out before you spend the days.

For my writing business, the quality of the content matters more than the design of the website. Both deserve attention, but improving the design won’t make an unhelpful article useful. It’s easy to spend time adjusting things you can see while avoiding the harder work of making something worth reading.

So write down the result in one sentence. Then ask which actions have a direct effect on it.

You might find that a lot of the work you planned isn’t necessary yet.

## 3. What are you willing to delay?

I learned this lesson when the pandemic started. I had an idea for a course about remote work. A few people responded positively, and I started creating it.

But I was already working on a course about starting an online business. Readers had asked for that one. I put it aside for nearly three months while I made the remote-work course, and the business course eventually got a better response.

I did the work and finished the new course. But I had chosen the wrong thing to do first.

That’s [opportunity cost](https://dariusforoux.com/opportunity-cost/). The time you spend on one thing is time you can’t spend on something else. And it’s easy to forget when the new thing sounds exciting.

Before committing to something, name what it will replace. If your answer is that you’ll fit it in somehow, look at your calendar again.

You don’t need to reject every new idea forever. Write it down and come back to it when you have room. But stop treating every idea as something you’ve already promised to do.

The same applies to work you’ve started. Some of it deserves to continue, some can wait, and some no longer makes sense. You don’t have to finish something just to prove you can finish it.

If other people are counting on you, discuss the change with them. You can’t secretly drop a commitment and call it prioritizing. Explain the trade-off and agree on what comes first.

## 4. Where does the important work fit in your week?

In 2017, I wanted to write a book about pragmatic thinking. Then I looked at my calendar and realized I hadn’t been scheduling time to write it.

Very impressive. A writing goal without any writing time.

That’s why I find a calendar useful. You can say something is important all you want. At some point, it needs a place in your day.

Start with your actual responsibilities. Your job, classes, family, appointments, whatever you already need to do. Then look at the time and energy you have left. Plan from there. Don’t make a schedule for someone who has no interruptions and never gets tired.

Here are a few things that help:

- Give demanding work a block of time when you tend to focus well. That might be in the morning or later in the day.
    
- Decide what you’ll work on before the block starts. “Draft the introduction” is more useful than “work on the article.”
    
- Put routine admin together where possible, so it doesn’t keep interrupting other work.
    
- Leave room between commitments. One delay shouldn’t ruin the entire day.
    

And make the block realistic. If you have a day job, a family, and a personal project, an hour you can regularly use is better than planning four hours you almost never have.

If you work in a role where other people set most of your schedule, discuss priorities with them. “I can finish this today, but then that will move to tomorrow. Which one do you need first?” That’s a useful conversation to have.

You can’t always control what comes in. You can still make the trade-offs clear.

## 5. What did the work produce?

At the end of the week, look at your original goal and what actually happened.

Did you get closer? What helped? What took a lot of time without doing much for you?

Be careful about judging yourself only on results you can’t fully control. You can send a good proposal and still lose the job. You can write a useful article and get fewer readers than you expected. That doesn’t automatically mean the work was bad.

But you also don’t want to use effort as an excuse forever. If something repeatedly produces very little, look at your approach.

Let’s say your goal is to find clients. You spend a week improving your website and don’t contact anyone. The following week, you talk to five potential clients and two ask for a proposal. That’s useful information about where your attention should go next.

Or you send proposals and never hear back. Before sending fifty more, ask someone to look at your offer. Maybe you’re approaching the wrong people, maybe the value isn’t clear, or maybe you need more proof that you can do the work.

That’s how you improve your priorities. You try something, pay attention to the response, and adjust. Give the work enough time to show you something, but don’t keep repeating it simply because it’s on your calendar.

## What will you do next week?

Take a page and write down:

1. The result that matters most this week.
    
2. The two or three actions most likely to help you get it.
    
3. When you’ll do them.
    
4. What you’ll delay or stop to make room.
    

Keep the bills, appointments, and other things you need to maintain on your calendar too. This page is there to help you see the work you want to move forward.

Then, at the end of the week, compare the plan with what happened. You might need more time, a smaller goal, or a different approach.

I still get excited about new ideas and have to correct myself. Knowing how to prioritize doesn’t make you immune to distraction. But it helps you notice when you’ve spent three days on something while the work you care about sits untouched.

When you notice that, change your next day. Make time for the thing you keep saying matters.
